At this time of year, it's not usually viable to hang laundry outside to dry.
My house doesn't have a good space indoors to dry laundry, and we've found that if clothes dont dry completely within about 24 hours, they start to smell damp and need re-washing, which is a big waste of water and energy.

Several years ago, we got a [plug-in heated drying rack](https://www.johnlewis.com/john-lewis-3-tier-heated-indoor-clothes-airer/p2427279),
and have been using this to speed up the drying process. This helps a lot and we've eliminated re-washing of clothes.
However, we occasionally want things dry quickly, and we find that our towels are left scratchy when hung to dry, inside or out.

As part of a kitchen renovation, we recently bought a [new washer-dryer](https://www.hotpoint.co.uk/hotpoint-ndb9635wuk-white-9plus6kg-washer-dryer-freestanding-f165186/p).
This gives us the option of tumble-drying clothes when we want to. This model has a 9Kg/6Kg wash/dry capacity. 
It's energy efficiency rating is "B" for the wash cycle and "D" for the dry cycle.
This is fairly typical, but more efficient machines are available.

The washer-dryer has a preset "Wash and Dry mixed" cycle which does a 30 degree wash, with a 1200rpm
spin cycle, and an "A3" dry cycle which is meant to leave clothes "cupboard dry" and ready to put away.
This cycle will process 3Kg of laundry, which is about the same as the amount as it takes to fully 
load the heated drying rack.

My assumption is that tumble drying is far more energy-intensive than the drying rack, so I am reluctant
to use it unless we have a particular need, but to help be judge the trade-off of cost vs convenience,
I wanted to know how much more energy the machine actually uses.

I put the machine on the "Wash and Dry mixed" cycle while plugged in to a Tapo P110 smart plug,
which has energy monitoring. The graph below shows the energy usage through the cycle,
which began at 8am and ended at 12pm.

The first hour is the majority of the wash cycle, which consumed about 0.25 KwH.

The second hour is the end of the wash cycle and the start of the drying, which consumed about 0.7 kWH. Presumably this is when most of the heat
is produced for drying.

The third hour is more drying, which consumed just over 0.4 kWH.

The fourth and final hour used just 0.06 kWH. I believe this is the "cool down" time, in which the washing continues to tumble but no further
heat is added.

This gives us a total of roughtly 1.5 kWH over 4 hours.

By comparison, the heated drying rack consumes a steady 300W. We typically run it for 6 hours overnight, when we get cheaper electricity.

This gives us a total of 0.3 x 6 = 1.8 kWH, which doesn't include the wash. If we add the 0.25 from the wash cycle, that's just over 2 kWH for a wash
and dry with the trying rack, a full 0.5 kWH

In both cases, there are usually one or two items that are not fully dry and we will hang them up to finish drying passively.

I was really surprised to see that this went so strongly against my assumption. Not only is the machine faster or more convenient, but it also
uses *less* energy overall. For clothes that are safe to tumble dry, this will be my default option during the colder months from now on.
